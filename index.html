What you're hearing

One signal (0 to 100) drives every sound: higher is brighter, faster and higher in pitch; lower is darker, slower and lower, with more echo and crackle. The mapping is a design choice, not a physical law.

NASA Earth Information Center (EIC)
NASA EIC visual frame-→NASA data at your pin-→Sound-

NASA's VEDA documentation says the VEDA dashboard powers Earth.gov (the Earth Information Center's website) and that Earth.gov uses the same VEDA data services. This mode loads datasets live from the VEDA catalogue (openveda.cloud), draws each frame on the globe with NASA's own colours, reads the same dataset's value at your pin, and turns it into sound. It lists VEDA datasets with a global map and a record longer than two years; that may not match the dashboard's own topic list exactly.

Each frame's value at your pin is compared with that pin's own average and spread over the frames shown (a z-score). Three standard deviations either side spans the whole sound range. The direction is a design choice.

About this data (read before citing)
  • Pictures: NASA GIBS (EOSDIS) tiles. Visible Earth combines up to three satellites (Suomi NPP, NOAA-20, NOAA-21) on the same day to fill gaps; any area still empty shows NASA Blue Marble and is reported as partial coverage.
  • Music numbers: NASA POWER monthly data, which come from the MERRA-2 reanalysis (a model that assimilates satellite and ground observations), not direct measurements at your pin. Whole Earth uses NASA GISS GISTEMP.
  • Climate signal: a prototype sonification score invented for this project, not an official NASA product and not a measure of ecological health. 50 = this place's 1981-2010 normal (Whole Earth: 1951-1980).
  • Colours versus warming: picture colours show actual temperatures, which swing by tens of degrees between seasons and places. A degree or two of warming over decades is nearly invisible in them. The graph and the music carry the long-term trend.
  • EIC mode: frames and values come live from NASA's VEDA catalogue (openveda.cloud), the data services behind the EIC dashboard. Values are read at your pin from the same data as the picture. Raw units are shown as NASA publishes them; see each dataset's description. NASA's VEDA documentation (docs.openveda.cloud, VEDA Dashboard page) says Earth.gov uses these same services, but the catalogue also serves other dashboards, so not every listed dataset is necessarily shown on earth.gov.
  • Moon facts: the "People and the Moon" list is unverified, from a Google AI overview.

The Moon

About 384,400 km from Earth. Drawn closer and larger than true scale.

The same face of the Moon always points at Earth. With almost no air, weather or plate tectonics, marks left on its surface last a very long time. Drag to look around, scroll to zoom, click Earth in the sky (or press Esc) to go back.

People and the Moon

Scientists have proposed a "Lunar Anthropocene". This summary comes from the Google AI overview you shared, which cites BBC, The Conversation, Live Science and EOS. I have not checked each figure against those sources.

  • Debris: over 227,000 kg (500,000 lb) of human-made material, such as landers, rovers, crashed probes and equipment, sits on the surface.
  • Footprints: Apollo-era footprints and rover tracks could last millions of years.
  • First impact: human-driven surface change began in 1959, when the Soviet Luna 2 probe hit the Moon on purpose.
  • Temporary atmospheres: landings and rocket exhaust can briefly thicken the thin lunar exosphere by up to 100,000 times, locally.
  • Contamination: escaped gas, water vapour and waste may freeze inside permanently shadowed craters and alter pristine samples.

What NASA has seen change

NASA does not publish a day-by-day Moon time-lapse like it does for Earth. These are individual before and after finds by the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LRO).

  • LRO's camera team reports at least about 1,000 new impact craters and around 100,000 other surface changes since 2009 (reported figure). LROC
  • The largest new crater found so far is about 222 m wide and formed in 2024. LROC
  • Russia's Luna 25 crashed on 24 Aug 2023 and LRO imaged the new crater. NASA
  • A spent SpaceX Falcon 9 upper stage hit the Moon in August 2026, and LRO imaged the new crater. NASA Science
  • Spent Apollo rocket stages left craters about 35 to 40 m wide. NASA
